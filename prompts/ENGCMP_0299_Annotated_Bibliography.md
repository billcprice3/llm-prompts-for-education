# LC3 Step 2 - Annotated Bibliography

## Taking Inventory Before You Paint

Throughout this course, we've practiced composing pieces of writing that position our ideas among other views, and that do so with precision and nuance. Your final Long Composition asks you to do this more fully than ever as you build your personal working philosophy of authorship and AI out of your own experience and a range of outside evidence.

You can think of drafting a composition as painting a picture. The brush strokes are yours: the ideas, the structure, the voice, the choices about what goes where. But to make the picture work, you need a palette of different colors that perform different jobs. Some sources might give you the scientific evidence; others might be emotionally evocative, ethically provocative, or philosophically inspiring.

An **annotated bibliography** is like taking inventory of your paints before you start painting. It's a list of your sources, each with a short description of what it says and how you might use it. What have you found so far? What does each source contribute? And how might all of them together help you paint the picture you're imagining?

## Part 1: Gather Your Sources

Collect **at least 5 sources** for your composition in your Zotero library. A good mix might include:

- readings, videos, and examples we've used in this course (look back over the modules, and at your LC3 Step 1 brainstorm);
- sources you find on your own, through the PittCat library catalog, web searches, news, video essays, and so on.

If you're not sure where to look, try the **Literature Mapping** prompt again with your LC3 topic to discover relevant fields and search queries.

## Part 2: Set Up a Gemini Notebook

**Gemini Notebook** (formerly called NotebookLM) is one of the AI tools available through Generative AI @ Pitt. Unlike a general-purpose chatbot, it's designed especially for source-based academic work. The basic idea is that you give it your own documents, and through the process of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (see Competence vs. Performance: Do Large Language Models Actually Know Anything?), Gemini Notebook answers questions about your sources, with citations that link back to specific passages.

Like everything we've explored this semester, this is new and experimental. Treat the tool's output the way we've treated every AI output in this course: as a starting point for your own exploration, at best a tertiary source, and not as an authority.

1. Open Gemini Notebook and sign in with your Pitt account.
2. Create a new notebook for LC3.
3. Add your sources to the notebook's **Sources** area. Where you can, upload the full text (such as a PDF of an article; keep in mind that you can create PDF files of web sources, news articles, etc by going to the `Print` screen and saving it as a PDF). For source types you can't upload directly, such as a video or a full book, you can add a note containing its APA-style reference list entry.

## Part 3: Generate an AI-Assisted Annotated Literature Review

Copy the prompt below into the notebook's chat, filling in the three bracketed parts. (Suggestions for LC3 are in parentheses, but feel free to make them your own.)

> I am working on a project about [TOPIC] *(e.g., "the role of AI tools in my own writing and thinking")*. What I am producing for this project is [OUTPUT AND CONTEXT] *(e.g., "a personal essay, a working philosophy of authorship and AI, for a first-year university writing seminar called Authorship and AI")*. The intended audience for this work is [AUDIENCE] *(e.g., "my instructor, my classmates, and myself")*.

> I have added my sources to this notebook. Some may be uploaded as full texts. Others might appear only as reference list entries, without their full text.

> Your task is to write a structured, annotated literature review of these sources, focused on how each one could support my project.

> Use the following structure:

> 1. INTRODUCTION
In one short paragraph, summarize the main themes that connect these sources and how, together, they relate to my project.

> 2. SOURCE-BY-SOURCE REVIEW
For each source, provide:
> - Reference: a reference list entry in APA 7th edition style.
> - Summary: two to four sentences on the source's main argument or findings.
> - Key concepts: the most important terms or ideas from the source that are relevant to my project. Be concise.
> - Relevance to my project: how this source could support, extend, or complicate my work. Be concise.
> - Possible use: where or how I might draw on it (for example, framing the problem, justifying the activity design, or interpreting results). Be concise.
> - Limitations (optional): any ways this source may not fit my project well. Be concise.

> Ground your summaries in the uploaded texts, and cite specific passages wherever possible. For any source that appears only as a reference entry without full text, clearly label it as "Reference only, full text not provided." For those sources, you can rely on what is widely known about the work, and keep that section brief. Do not invent quotations, page numbers, or findings for any source.

> 3. SUGGESTIONS
Offer a few practical recommendations for how I might combine these sources in my project. For example, which sources pair well? Which concepts could anchor my argument? Are there any useful connections between sources I might not have noticed?

> 4. POINTS OF CONNECTION TO CONSIDER
Identify a small number of high-priority perspectives, fields, or types of sources that seem missing and would fit my project especially well. This section is not meant as a critique or as bikeshedding, but purely generative, as of a colleague at the water cooler saying, "You know, that reminds me of...". For each potential blind spot, explain briefly why it might matter, and suggest two or three search queries I could use in a university library catalog to find relevant scholarly sources. Do not name specific articles or books unless you are confident they exist; it is better to suggest search terms than to risk recommending a source that does not exist.

## Part 4: Reflect

Read through the output carefully. Try clicking on a few of its citations to see the passages it's drawing from.

Then, in your own words, write a short reflection (about 150 to 250 words) responding to some of these questions:

- Did Gemini's summaries and suggestions match what was already on your mind about your sources, or did anything differ or surprise you?
- When you clicked on its citations, did its summaries seem accurate to the original sources?
- Do you feel more clarity, or less clarity, about the direction of your composition now?
- Do you feel like Gemini is "doing the work for you," or do you still feel like you're in the driver's seat? Why?
